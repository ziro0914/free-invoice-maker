Milestone Billing for Freelancers: How to Split a Project Fee and Invoice Each Stage

Milestone billing means splitting a project fee into several payments, each tied to a clearly defined stage of the work. It suits projects that run longer than a couple of weeks, where invoicing everything at the end leaves you carrying the cost for too long. This guide covers how to choose milestones, how to split the fee, how to word each invoice, and the mistakes that cause disputes. You can build each invoice with our free invoice generator.

When milestone billing makes sense

Freelancer guides from payment and invoicing vendors commonly suggest a single invoice (or a 50/50 deposit and completion split) for short, simple jobs, and milestones once a project stretches over several weeks or has distinct phases. There is no fixed threshold, so use your judgement:

  • Use milestones for multi-week projects, work with several deliverables, or clients you have not worked with before.
  • Skip milestones for a one-day job or a small fixed-price task where the paperwork costs more than the risk.
  • Consider monthly billing instead for open-ended work with no natural end point, such as ongoing support.

Choose milestones that are about deliverables, not dates

A milestone should be something both sides can point at and agree is finished. "Design approved" is clear. "Halfway through" is not. Advice from freelance resources generally favors defining milestones by deliverables and approvals rather than by calendar dates alone, because dates slip when a client is slow to give feedback.

Good milestone triggers include:

  1. Signed contract and deposit received (the project start).
  2. First draft, mockup or prototype delivered.
  3. Client feedback received and revisions completed.
  4. Final files delivered or the work goes live.

Two to four payment points is a common range. More than that and you spend your time invoicing instead of working.

How to split the fee

There is no official formula. Practitioner examples often look like the ones below. They are illustrations, not surveys, so adjust them to the project and the client.

Project typeExample split
Short project, new client50% to start, 50% on delivery
Standard multi-week project30% to start, 40% after first draft, 30% on final delivery
Larger project with phases25% to start, then equal payments at each phase, with the last one on completion

Keep the last payment large enough that the client still has a reason to approve the work promptly. Many freelancers keep the final files until the final invoice is settled, though what is enforceable depends on your contract and local rules, so check that before relying on it.

A worked example

Say you quote a website build at $6,000 with a 30/40/30 split:

  • Milestone 1 (contract signed): 30% of $6,000 = $1,800
  • Milestone 2 (first draft delivered): 40% of $6,000 = $2,400
  • Milestone 3 (final delivery): 30% of $6,000 = $1,800

The three payments add up to $6,000. If you are unsure what the total should be in the first place, try the freelance project price calculator before you quote.

Put it in writing before you start

Milestone billing only works if the terms are agreed up front. Your contract or proposal should state:

  • The total fee and the amount of each payment.
  • What counts as completing each milestone, and who approves it.
  • How long the client has to review and approve (for example, a set number of business days), and what happens if they do not respond.
  • The payment terms for each invoice, such as Net 7 or Net 14. See our guide to invoice payment terms.
  • What happens if the project is cancelled partway, including whether paid milestones are refundable. Wording and enforceability vary by location, so consider getting a template reviewed by a professional.

How to write a milestone invoice

Each invoice should make clear which stage it covers and how it fits into the whole. Useful details to include:

  • A line describing the milestone, for example "Milestone 2 of 3: first draft delivered".
  • The milestone amount, plus any tax if it applies to you.
  • The total project fee and what has been invoiced so far, in the notes field, so the client can see the balance.
  • A unique invoice number. Our invoice numbering guide explains simple systems that stay tidy across several invoices per client.
  • The due date and your payment instructions.

If you took a deposit first, treat it as milestone one, or show it as a credit on the final invoice as described in our deposit invoice guide. Do not count it twice.

Sample wording for your proposal

The total project fee is $6,000, invoiced in three parts: 30% on signing, 40% when the first draft is delivered, and 30% on final delivery. Each invoice is due within 14 days. Work on the next stage begins once the previous invoice is paid.

That last sentence matters. Tying the start of the next stage to payment of the previous invoice is a simple way to avoid building up unpaid work, as long as the client has agreed to it.

Common mistakes

  • Vague milestones. "Phase two" can mean different things to you and the client. Name the deliverable.
  • No approval window. If a client can sit on a draft forever, your payment waits forever. Set a review period in the contract.
  • Back-loading the payments. Putting most of the fee at the end is the same risk as invoicing at the end, just with a small deposit on top.
  • Forgetting scope changes. Extra work outside the agreed scope should be quoted and invoiced separately, not folded silently into a milestone.
  • Skipping reminders. More invoices means more due dates to track. If one is late, our late payment email templates and late fee guide can help.

Quick checklist

  1. Decide whether the project is long enough to need milestones.
  2. Pick two to four deliverable-based milestones.
  3. Split the fee and check that the parts add up to the total.
  4. Write the milestones, approval window and payment terms into your contract.
  5. Invoice at each milestone with a clear description and number.
