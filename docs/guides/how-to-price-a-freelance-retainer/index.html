How to Price and Invoice a Freelance Retainer (Rollover, Overage, Clauses)

A retainer turns one-off jobs into predictable monthly income, but only if the terms are clear. Most disputes come from vague wording about hours, rollover and extra work. This guide covers how to price a retainer, which clauses to write down, and how to invoice it each month. What a retainer actually is

A retainer is a recurring payment, usually monthly, that reserves a block of your time or a defined set of deliverables for a client. The client gets priority access and a predictable cost. You get steadier income and fewer sales conversations. Practices vary by industry, so treat the figures below as common conventions rather than rules.

Step 1: Price it from your hourly rate

Start with the hours the client wants reserved and your standard hourly rate. If you are unsure of your rate, see the hourly rate calculator.

  • Reserved hours × standard rate = full value of the block.
  • Optionally subtract a commitment discount. Many freelancers use something in the range of 5-15%, though some charge a premium for guaranteed availability instead.

Example: 20 hours at $100 is $2,000. With a 10% discount the monthly retainer is $1,800, an effective $90 per hour.

Step 2: Define the scope in writing

State either the hours or the deliverables covered each month, for example "up to 20 hours of design work" or "four blog posts and one newsletter". Vague scope is the most common source of arguments. Say what is not included, and how new requests are handled.

Step 3: Decide what happens to unused hours

There are three common options:

  • No rollover: unused hours expire at month end. Simple, and you are paid for reserving your time.
  • Capped rollover: some hours carry over, up to a limit (for example a quarter of the monthly block), and expire after a set window.
  • Expiry window: unused hours stay valid for a fixed number of days, then lapse.

Any of these can work. What matters is that you pick one and write it into both the agreement and each invoice, so nobody assumes otherwise.

Step 4: Set the rule for extra work

Work beyond the reserved block should be quoted or billed separately. Some freelancers bill overage at their full standard rate, others add a small premium because the work interrupts planned capacity. State which you use and whether extra work needs approval first.

Example: The $1,800 retainer covers 20 hours. In a busy month the client needs 3 extra hours at your full $100 rate: 3 × $100 = $300. The month's invoice is $1,800 + $300 = $2,100.

Step 5: Add the practical clauses

  • Billing date: many freelancers invoice in advance, at or just before the start of the period, so the retainer works as a reservation fee.
  • Payment terms: see our guide to invoice payment terms and late fees.
  • Notice period: a written notice period for ending or changing the retainer (30 days is a frequent choice) and what happens to the final invoice.
  • Review point: a quarterly check on whether the hours and price still fit.

Contract rules differ by country and by state or province, so consider having a lawyer review your agreement template once.

How to invoice a retainer

A clear monthly retainer invoice usually has:

  • A consistent invoice number series, for example RET-2026-11.
  • The exact period covered, with start and end dates.
  • One line for the retainer fee and the hours it includes.
  • Separate lines for any overage, with hours and rate.
  • A note repeating the rollover rule.
  • The due date and your payment details.

Common mistakes

  • Setting the retainer from a wishful hourly rate instead of your real costs and utilisation.
  • Letting small extras slide until the retainer quietly becomes unlimited work.
  • Leaving rollover unwritten, then disagreeing about it months later.
  • Invoicing in arrears with long terms, which makes the retainer a loan to your client.
  • Never reviewing it: scope and your rates both change.

Is a retainer right for this client?

Retainers suit clients with steady, recurring needs such as ongoing design tweaks, content, bookkeeping, support or advice. They fit poorly when the work is a single large project, where milestone billing or a deposit is usually cleaner. If you are unsure, offer a short trial of two or three months with a clear review date before committing either side long term.

Protecting your time

Track hours against the block as you go and share a short monthly summary with the client. It shows the value delivered, makes overage conversations easy because the numbers are already on the table, and gives you evidence if the scope drifts. Be realistic about capacity too: a retainer reserves hours, so avoid selling more blocks than your week can hold alongside project work and admin.

Checklist

  • Hours or deliverables defined, with exclusions.
  • Monthly fee and any discount stated.
  • Rollover rule chosen and written down.
  • Overage rate and approval process set.
  • Billing date, due date and notice period agreed.
  • Review date in the calendar.
