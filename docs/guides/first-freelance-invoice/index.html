How to Send Your First Invoice as a Freelancer

Sending your first invoice as a freelancer can feel oddly formal. Nobody hands you a rulebook, and it is easy to worry that you will leave something out or look unprofessional. The good news is that a clear invoice is mostly a checklist. This guide walks through what to include, when to send it and how to avoid the mistakes that slow payment down. Before you invoice: agree the basics

An invoice should never be a surprise. Before you start work, confirm in writing (an email is fine) the scope of the work, your rate or fixed price, any deposit, and when payment is due. When the invoice later matches what was agreed, there is very little for the client to question. For larger projects, a simple contract or statement of work is worth having.

What every freelance invoice should include

  1. Your details: your name or business name, address, email and, if you have one, a logo.
  2. Client details: the client's business name and address, plus the name of the person who handles payments if you know it.
  3. A unique invoice number: a simple running sequence such as 001, 002, 003 works. Numbers make it easy to refer to a specific invoice and to track what has been paid.
  4. Dates: the date you issued the invoice and the date payment is due.
  5. Line items: a short description of each piece of work, the quantity or hours, the rate and the line total.
  6. Tax and total: any sales tax, VAT or GST (if you are required to charge it), then the final amount due.
  7. Payment instructions: how the client can pay you, such as bank transfer details, a payment link or an address for cheques.
  8. Terms and notes: your payment terms, any late-fee wording, and a brief thank-you.

Writing line items clients understand

Be specific enough that someone in the client's accounts department, who may not know you, can see what they are paying for. "Website design" is vague. "Homepage and two inner page designs, including two revision rounds" is clear. For hourly work, show hours and your hourly rate. For fixed-price work, one line per deliverable or milestone is usually easier to read than a single lump sum.

Choosing payment terms

Payment terms tell the client when to pay. Common choices include "due on receipt", net 14 and net 30. Shorter terms help your cash flow, but larger companies often have their own payment cycles, so ask what theirs is. Whatever you choose, put it on the invoice in plain words and the due date next to it. Our guide to invoice payment terms goes deeper on the options.

Taxes: what to check

Whether you must charge sales tax, VAT or GST depends on where you and your client are, what you sell and sometimes your turnover. Rules differ between countries, states and provinces, and they change. This site cannot give tax advice, so check with your local tax authority or an accountant before you decide. If you do charge tax, show it as a separate line so the client can see the amount. Also remember that income from invoices is generally reportable, so keep copies of everything you send.

Sending the invoice

  • Send a PDF by email, with a short, friendly message and the due date in the body.
  • Send it promptly after the work is delivered or the milestone is reached. Delay usually means delayed payment.
  • Use a clear subject line, for example "Invoice 001 from Your Name, due 30 November".
  • Keep a copy and note the date you sent it, so you know when to follow up.

Common first-invoice mistakes

  • Leaving off the due date, so the client has no deadline.
  • Forgetting payment details and making the client email back to ask.
  • Reusing an invoice number or skipping around in the sequence.
  • Lumping everything into one line that invites questions.
  • Mixing up estimates, invoices and receipts. If you are unsure, read invoice vs receipt.

What if the client is late?

It happens to nearly every freelancer. Send a polite reminder a day or two after the due date, then follow up at sensible intervals. We have copy-and-paste wording in our late payment email templates. Stating your late-fee terms on the invoice up front makes these conversations easier, but check local rules on what fees are allowed.

A quick checklist

  1. Scope and price agreed in writing.
  2. Unique invoice number and both dates.
  3. Specific line items with quantities and rates.
  4. Tax shown separately, if it applies to you.
  5. Payment instructions and terms in plain words.
  6. PDF sent promptly with a clear subject line.

Your first invoice will not be perfect, and it does not need to be. Clear, complete and on time beats beautiful but missing a due date. After a couple of invoices the process becomes routine.
