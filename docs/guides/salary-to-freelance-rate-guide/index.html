How to Convert a Salary to a Freelance Rate (With Example)

Moving from a job to freelancing, the first number most people reach for is their salary divided by 2,080 hours. It feels logical, and it is almost always too low. This guide explains why, walks through a worked example, and shows how to build a freelance rate that actually replaces your salary. You can run your own figures in the salary to freelance rate calculator, then bill clients with the free invoice generator.

Why salary divided by 2,080 is too low

2,080 is 40 hours x 52 weeks. That number assumes every hour is paid, which is true for an employee with paid holidays and sick days, but not for a freelancer. As a freelancer you stop being paid for several things at once:

  • Time off: holidays, sick days and vacation are unpaid unless you price them in.
  • Non-billable time: finding clients, sending proposals, bookkeeping, email and chasing late payments all take hours that never appear on an invoice.
  • Benefits: health cover, retirement contributions and similar extras an employer paid for are now your cost.
  • Payroll taxes: in many countries an employer pays a share of payroll or social contributions on top of your salary, while a self-employed person usually pays the full amount. Rules differ, so check local rules.
  • Business costs: software, equipment, insurance, a workspace and fees.

The formula

A common approach used by many rate guides is:

Freelance hourly rate = (salary + benefits + payroll or self-employment taxes + business costs) / billable hours per year

The key is the last part. Billable hours are not 2,080. They are the weeks you actually work, times your hours per week, times the share of those hours you can charge for.

A worked example

Suppose your target is a 70,000 salary. These percentages are placeholders for illustration, not statistics, so swap in your own.

  1. Replace benefits and employer-side tax. Assume 20% for benefits and 7.65% for the payroll or self-employment tax share you now cover. That is 27.65% of 70,000, so 70,000 x 1.2765 = 89,355.
  2. Add business costs. Say 4,000 a year for software, gear and insurance: 89,355 + 4,000 = 93,355 of revenue needed.
  3. Work out billable hours. Take 6 weeks off, so 46 working weeks. At 40 hours a week that is 1,840 hours. If 65% of them are billable, that is 46 x 40 x 0.65 = 1,196 billable hours.
  4. Divide. 93,355 / 1,196 = about 78.06 per hour.

Compare that with the naive version: 70,000 / 2,080 = about 33.65 per hour. The freelance rate is roughly 2.32 times higher. That surprises many people, but notice that no single step is extravagant. The jump comes from stacking several real costs and from billing only about two-thirds of your hours.

Do not forget income tax

The example above covers the extras an employer used to pay. It does not include income tax on your profit, which depends on your country, region and situation. Think of the result as the revenue you need to reach a salary-equivalent income before income tax is dealt with in the same way it was for your job. A tax professional or your local tax authority can tell you what applies to you. If you want to estimate a set-aside amount, try the self-employed tax set-aside calculator.

Check the rate against hours and projects

An hourly figure is a starting point, not a promise. Two checks help:

  • Day rate: if you sell by the day, multiply by your working hours and compare it with the day rate calculator.
  • Project price: estimate the hours a project takes, multiply by your rate, and add a buffer for revisions. The project price calculator helps, and the scope creep guide explains how to handle extra requests.

Once you are working, track your real numbers. The effective hourly rate calculator and the guide on effective hourly rate vs billing rate show what you really earn per hour after unpaid time.

Common mistakes

  • Assuming 100% billable time. Most new freelancers overestimate this. Start conservative and adjust with your own records.
  • Forgetting unpaid time off. Six weeks may sound like a lot until you count holidays and sick days.
  • Anchoring on the market rate alone. Market rates matter, but if your needed rate is far above what clients pay in your niche, you may need to reduce costs, raise your billable share, or specialise.
  • Discounting too early. A low first rate is hard to raise with the same client later.

When the number looks too high

If the result feels out of reach, change one input at a time and see what moves it. Cutting costs by 1,000 helps a little; lifting the billable share from 65% to 75% helps far more, because it spreads the same revenue need across more hours. Specialising, packaging a repeatable service and keeping a steady client base all work on that billable share. Use the calculator to test each scenario before you commit to a price.

Ways to raise the result without raising prices

  • Increase the billable share by batching admin and saving a template for proposals and invoices.
  • Move from hourly billing to retainers or fixed prices for repeat work.
  • Ask for a deposit and use clear payment terms so late payments do not eat unpaid hours.
