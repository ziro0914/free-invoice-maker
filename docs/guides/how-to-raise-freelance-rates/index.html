How to Raise Your Freelance Rates Without Losing Clients

Most freelancers set a rate once and then leave it alone for years while costs, skills and experience all grow. Raising your rate is one of the fastest ways to lift income without working more hours, yet it is the thing people put off longest. This guide covers when to raise, how much, how to tell existing clients, and how to bill the change cleanly. Signs it is time to raise your rate

  • You are fully booked. If you turn work away or have a waiting list, demand is higher than your price reflects.
  • You have not changed your rate in a year or more. Many guides suggest reviewing rates at least once a year; this is a rule of thumb, not a law.
  • Your effective rate is below target. Once unpaid admin time is counted, your real hourly income may be well under your billing rate. See the effective rate guide.
  • Your skills or results have grown. New certifications, a stronger portfolio or faster delivery are all valid reasons.
  • Your costs have risen. Software, insurance, equipment and living costs tend to creep up.
  • Clients never push back. If every quote is accepted instantly, you may be priced too low.

How much to raise

Sources vary, and some suggest a 10 to 20 percent annual adjustment as common. Treat any percentage as a starting point and decide based on your own numbers. A simple way is to pick a target effective rate, work backwards to the billing rate, and compare it with what you charge now.

Example with placeholder figures: you bill $60 an hour. A 10 percent increase gives $66 (60 x 1.10). A 15 percent increase gives $69 (60 x 1.15). Even a small step of 3 percent gives $61.80, which is a way to at least keep pace with rising costs.

The break-even check

Worry about losing clients is the main reason people avoid raising rates, so do the arithmetic. If you raise from $60 to $66, you earn the same total income even if you lose about 9 percent of your billable hours (60 / 66 = 0.909). At $69, you could lose about 13 percent of hours and still earn the same (60 / 69 = 0.870). Fewer hours at a higher rate also means less work for the same money, which leaves room to find better clients. This ignores taxes and any costs that scale with hours, so adjust for your own situation.

Who to raise first

  1. New clients. They have no old price to compare with, so quote the new rate from today.
  2. Clients that cost you the most effort. Slow payers, scope-creepers and heavy revision requesters are the first ones to reprice.
  3. Long-standing clients. Give them notice and, if you like, a smaller increase as thanks for loyalty.

Sources disagree on whether existing clients should get the full increase or a reduced one. Either approach is reasonable as long as you are consistent and clear.

How to tell existing clients

Common advice across freelance guides is to give written notice, often 30 to 60 days, and longer for retainer clients who may need budget approval. Send it as its own email with a clear subject line, keep it short and factual, and state the effective date. You are informing, not asking permission, so there is no need to apologise.

Subject: Rate update effective 1 February
Hi Sam, thank you for the work together this year. From 1 February my hourly rate will be $66. Projects already agreed and in progress will stay at the current rate. Happy to talk through any questions.

Tie the change to value where you can: results delivered, new skills, faster turnaround. Avoid blaming vague market forces unless that is the real reason.

Protect work already agreed

Changing a price in the middle of an engagement is widely seen as unprofessional. Keep existing quotes and fixed-fee projects at the agreed price, and apply the new rate to new work and renewals. For monthly arrangements, see how to reprice a retainer, and for fixed-price jobs make sure change requests are billed at the new rate using the scope creep guide.

Put it on the invoice

  • Show the effective date and the rate on each line, so the change is never a surprise.
  • If a billing period spans the change date, split it into two lines, one at the old rate and one at the new rate.
  • Restate your payment terms while you are at it.
  • Keep a note of when each client was last repriced so the next review is easy.

Common mistakes

  • Raising by a tiny amount every time. A 1 percent nudge may not even cover costs. Pick a step that matters.
  • Apologising or over-explaining. Long justifications invite negotiation.
  • Raising too often. Some guides say frequent changes can look unstable for long-term clients; yearly is a common rhythm.
  • Not raising at all. Inflation and rising costs erode real income when your price stays flat.
  • Forgetting tax. A higher income may change what you should set aside. Use the tax set-aside calculator as a rough guide and check local rules.

A simple yearly routine

  1. Calculate your effective rate for the year with the effective hourly rate calculator.
  2. Set your target income and see what billing rate it needs with the salary to freelance rate calculator.
  3. Choose the new rate and the date it starts.
  4. Send notices 30 to 60 days ahead.
  5. Update your templates and invoices.
