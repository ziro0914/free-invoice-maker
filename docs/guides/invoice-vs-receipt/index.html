Invoice vs Receipt: What's the Difference?

Invoices and receipts get mixed up constantly, even by people who have been self-employed for years. They are both billing documents and they often look alike, but they do different jobs. Sending the wrong one can delay payment or leave you without proof of what happened. This guide explains the difference in plain terms, when to send each, and what to put on them.

The short answer

An invoice asks for money. A receipt confirms that money arrived. The usual order is invoice first, receipt second. The invoice looks forward ("here is what you owe and by when"); the receipt looks back ("thank you, this has been paid").

What an invoice should include

Requirements differ by country and by whether you are registered for sales tax or VAT, so check your local rules. In general, a complete invoice has:

  • The word "Invoice" and a unique invoice number
  • Your name or business name and contact details
  • Your client's name and billing details
  • The issue date and the payment due date
  • An itemised list of services or products with quantities and rates
  • Subtotal, any tax or discount, and the total due
  • Payment instructions and your payment terms

What a receipt should include

A receipt is simpler. It usually shows:

  • The word "Receipt" (or "Paid")
  • A receipt number, or the number of the invoice it settles
  • Your details and the client's details
  • The date the payment was received
  • The amount paid and the payment method (bank transfer, card, cash, cheque)
  • A short description of what was paid for
  • A balance of zero, or the remaining balance if it was a part payment

Can an invoice double as a receipt?

Often, yes. Many freelancers simply send the original invoice back marked "PAID", with the date and payment method added. That is a common and practical approach, and for most small jobs clients are happy with it. A few things keep it clean:

  1. Keep the same invoice number so the two documents are easy to match.
  2. Add the date payment was received, not just the word "paid".
  3. Show a balance due of zero.
  4. Keep a copy of both versions in your records.

Some clients, especially larger companies, ask for a separate receipt for their accounts department. Giving them one costs you two minutes and avoids an email chain.

When you should send each

Send an invoice when

  • You have finished the work and are waiting to be paid
  • A project milestone or deposit stage has been reached
  • You bill on a recurring schedule, such as monthly retainers

Send a receipt when

  • A client pays by cash, cheque or bank transfer and wants confirmation
  • A client pays in instalments and wants a record of each one
  • You take payment up front and there was never a separate invoice

Where "proforma" and "estimate" fit in

Two related documents cause extra confusion. An estimate or quote is a price proposal before work begins; it is not a demand for payment. A proforma invoice is a preliminary bill sent before delivery, often for deposits or international shipments. Neither is a receipt, and neither normally counts as your final tax invoice, so check how your local tax authority treats them.

Part payments and deposits

If a client pays a deposit, issue a receipt for that amount, then show it as a credit on the final invoice so the balance due is obvious. For example, if the total is 1,000 and the client paid a 300 deposit, the final invoice lists the full 1,000, subtracts the 300 already paid, and requests the remaining 700. This keeps both of you clear on what has and has not been settled.

Why this matters at tax time

Invoices show what you earned or are owed; receipts show what actually changed hands. Depending on whether you use cash or accrual accounting, which one counts toward your income in a given period can differ. This is general information, not tax advice, so check with a qualified accountant or your local tax authority about the rules that apply to you.

Keep both for as long as your local rules require. Many jurisdictions expect business records to be kept for several years, but the period varies, so confirm yours.

Common mistakes

  • Sending a receipt before the money arrives. Wait until funds have cleared, particularly for card payments and cheques.
  • Reusing invoice numbers. Each invoice needs a unique number, and sequential numbering is expected in some countries.
  • Leaving the payment date off a paid invoice. "Paid" without a date is weak proof.
  • Not saving copies. Store PDFs in a dated folder or in cloud storage you control.
  • Mixing up dates. The invoice date, due date and payment date are three different things.

A simple workflow

  1. Agree scope and price in writing.
  2. Deliver the work and send the invoice with clear payment terms.
  3. When payment arrives, mark the invoice paid or issue a receipt.
  4. File both copies together.
