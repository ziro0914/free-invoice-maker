What Is a Proforma Invoice? (And When You Need One)

If a client has ever asked you for a "proforma invoice", you may have wondered whether it is a real invoice, a quote, or something else entirely. It is mostly a preview: a document that shows the price and terms of a sale before the sale is final. This guide explains what a proforma invoice is, how it differs from a normal (commercial) invoice and from a quote, and when a freelancer or small business actually needs one.

What is a proforma invoice?

"Pro forma" is Latin for "as a matter of form". A proforma invoice tells the buyer what they would be charged if they go ahead: the items or services, the quantities, the prices, any tax or shipping, and the payment terms. It is not a demand for payment. Most accounting guides describe it as non-binding, and it normally does not get recorded as a sale in your books. It is also usually given no official invoice number, to keep it out of your normal invoice sequence.

The practical rule: a proforma shows what is going to be billed, and the commercial invoice is the bill. If you ever need to change figures after a commercial invoice has gone out, the usual route is a credit note or a corrected invoice rather than editing the original, so it is worth getting the numbers right on the proforma first.

Proforma invoice vs quote (estimate)

These two are easy to confuse. A quote or estimate is an offer: "I can do this job for this price." A proforma invoice is formatted like an invoice and is usually produced after a quote has been accepted, often because the buyer needs an invoice-shaped document for something else. In many everyday freelance situations a clear quote is all you need. Create a proforma only when someone asks for one.

When do you actually need one?

  • International shipments. A buyer may need a document showing the value of goods to apply for an import permit or to arrange a payment such as a letter of credit. Customs authorities decide what they accept, and the commercial invoice is generally the primary document, so check the destination country's requirements.
  • Buyer's internal approval. Some companies will not release a purchase order or budget without an invoice-style document showing the amount.
  • Bank transfers and financing. A buyer may need to show the amount to their bank before paying.
  • Custom or made-to-order work. A client can see the full cost and terms in invoice format before you start.
  • Samples and gifts. For goods sent without a sale, a proforma can show a value for customs purposes. Rules differ by country, so confirm locally.

Do not use a proforma to collect a deposit

If you want money up front, a proforma is usually the wrong tool. Because it is not a formal request for payment and normally stays out of your accounts, mixing it with a deposit gets messy. Send a clearly labelled deposit invoice instead (for example "Invoice 1042 - 50% deposit"), then issue a final invoice for the balance and show the deposit already received. How deposits are treated for tax can differ by location, so check the rules that apply to you or ask an accountant.

What to include on a proforma invoice

  1. The title "Proforma Invoice" in large, obvious letters, so nobody mistakes it for a bill
  2. Your business name, address and contact details
  3. The buyer's name and address
  4. The date issued and a validity period, such as "Prices valid for 30 days"
  5. A description of each item or service, with quantity and unit price
  6. Tax, shipping or other charges shown separately, or a note that they are excluded
  7. The currency
  8. Payment terms and the details you would use on the final invoice
  9. A short note such as "This is not a tax invoice and not a request for payment"

For goods that cross a border, add the country of origin, a clear description of the goods, and the shipping terms you have agreed. Ask the buyer or the customs authority which extra fields they need, because requirements vary.

Common mistakes

  • Using the same number sequence as real invoices, which leaves gaps or duplicates in your records.
  • Forgetting the expiry date, so a client tries to use an old price months later.
  • Not labelling it clearly, which can lead a client's accounts team to pay it, or to ignore it.
  • Treating it as the final bill. Always follow with the actual invoice once the sale is confirmed.

The bottom line

A proforma invoice previews a sale and confirms the price; the commercial invoice is the real bill. Most freelancers will rarely need one, but when a buyer asks, label it clearly, keep it out of your numbered invoice sequence, set an expiry date, and follow it with a proper invoice. Requirements for customs and tax differ from country to country, so check with the relevant authority or an accountant before relying on any document for those purposes.
