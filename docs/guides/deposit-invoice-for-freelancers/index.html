Deposit Invoices for Freelancers: How Much to Ask and How to Invoice It

Asking for money before you start can feel awkward, but a deposit is one of the simplest ways to protect your time and your cash flow. This guide explains deposits versus retainers, how much freelancers commonly ask for, how to show a deposit on an invoice, and how to word it. To build the invoice itself, use our free invoice generator.

Deposit vs retainer: what is the difference?

The two words are often used loosely, and the terms are not standardized. In general:

  • Deposit (upfront payment): a part of the project fee paid in advance for work not yet done. It is normally credited against the final invoice.
  • Retainer: a recurring payment that reserves your time or covers an ongoing amount of work, such as "10 hours a month". Some professions also use the word for a prepaid balance that is drawn down.
  • Booking fee: a label some freelancers use for a fee that pays for reserving a date or for setup time. It can describe the purpose more clearly than "deposit".

Whatever you call it, write down in your contract what the payment is for and what happens to it if the project is cancelled.

How much should you ask for?

There is no official rule. Advice from freelancer blogs and forums commonly lands in the range of 25% to 50% of the project fee, with a higher share for small projects and a lower share for large ones. Some freelancers split bigger jobs into milestones, for example a third at the start, a third at the midpoint and a third on delivery. These are rules of thumb from practitioners, not surveys, so adjust them to your field and your client.

SituationCommon approach
Small, short projectLarger share upfront (often up to half)
Large project over several weeksSmaller deposit plus milestone invoices
Ongoing monthly workRetainer billed at the start of each month
New client, no historyDeposit before work begins

How to show a deposit on an invoice

The cleanest method is two invoices: a deposit invoice at the start and a final invoice that credits the deposit. Here is a worked example for a $3,000 project with a 30% deposit.

  1. Deposit invoice: one line, "Project deposit (30% of $3,000)", total $900.
  2. Final invoice: list the full work at $3,000, then subtract "Deposit already paid" of $900. Balance due: $2,100.

Check: 3,000 x 0.30 = 900, and 3,000 - 900 = 2,100. Reference the deposit invoice number on the final one so your records line up, and keep your numbering consistent (see our guide to invoice numbering).

Refundable or non-refundable?

This is where wording matters, and it is also where local law can differ. Sources disagree on whether the label alone decides what is refundable. Many freelancers use a simple policy such as: fully refundable if you have not started work, non-refundable (or partly refundable) once work has begun, to cover the time already spent. Put the policy in writing before the client pays, and explain why. A clause that is clearly tied to work done or time reserved is generally easier to defend than a blanket "non-refundable" statement. Rules vary by place and profession, so check your local rules or ask a professional for anything high-value.

Keep in mind that a client can still dispute a card payment with their bank, so written acceptance of your terms is useful evidence.

Sample wording

A deposit of 30% of the project fee is due before work begins and will be credited against the final invoice. If the project is cancelled before work starts, the deposit is refunded. After work starts, the deposit covers time already spent and is not refunded.

Adapt this to your situation; it is a starting point, not legal advice.

Set the due date and payment terms

A deposit invoice usually has a short due date, such as "due on receipt" or within a few days, and work starts only once it is paid. For the final balance, choose terms that fit the client; our comparison of Net 30 vs Net 15 and the guide to invoice payment terms can help. Adding a clear late-payment clause is also wise; see how to charge late fees.

Tax note

A deposit is still income in most tax systems, even if the work is not finished, though the timing rules depend on your country and accounting method. Set aside part of it for taxes using our tax set-aside guide, and check local rules or ask an accountant how deposits are treated for you.

Handling pushback

Some clients will hesitate. Stay calm and explain that the deposit reserves your calendar and covers the first stage of work. If a client refuses any upfront payment, you can offer alternatives: a smaller deposit, shorter milestones, or invoicing the first stage immediately on delivery. For a first-time client, a modest deposit is usually easier to agree than a large one. Whatever you decide, treat every client the same way so your terms stay simple to manage, and see our freelance writer invoice template for an example layout you can adapt.

Checklist

  • Agree the deposit amount and what it covers in writing.
  • Invoice it with a clear description and a short due date.
  • Do not start until it is paid.
  • Credit it on the final invoice and reference the deposit invoice number.
  • Keep copies of the contract, invoices and payment confirmations.
