How to Charge Late Fees on Invoices (Wording, Rates and Legal Checks)

Late payment is one of the most common freelance headaches, and a late fee is one of the tools people reach for. Used well, it nudges clients to pay on time. Used badly, it sours a relationship or turns out to be unenforceable. This guide covers when a late fee makes sense, how to word it, how much freelancers typically charge, and the legal points worth checking before you rely on one.

Start with the rule that matters most: tell the client first

Most guides agree on one point: a late fee should be agreed before the work starts. Put it in your contract, proposal or written terms, and repeat it on every invoice. Adding a fee for the first time to an invoice that is already overdue is much harder to defend, and clients tend to push back, reasonably, that they never agreed to it.

  • State the due date clearly (for example "Due 30 June", not just "Net 30").
  • State the fee and how it is calculated.
  • Say when it starts (immediately after the due date, or after a grace period).

If you have not yet set clear terms, read our guide to invoice payment terms first, because the fee only makes sense on top of a clear due date.

How much do freelancers usually charge?

There is no single correct number. Many freelancer-focused guides mention something around 1% to 2% per month as a common range, and some suggest a flat minimum for small invoices. Treat those as rules of thumb rather than standards. A good test is whether the fee is high enough that the client takes it seriously but low enough that you would be comfortable explaining it to them face to face.

Check the legal limits where you and your client are based

This is the part people skip. Rules on late fees and interest differ a lot by country and, in the US, by state. Some places cap the rate, some require specific disclosure, and some treat a fee differently depending on whether the client is a business or a consumer. A few points to keep in mind:

  • United States: limits on interest and fees vary by state and by type of contract. Do not copy a percentage from a blog post without checking what applies to you.
  • United Kingdom: there is a statutory regime for late payment of commercial debts that can allow interest and fixed compensation on business-to-business invoices. The official gov.uk guidance has the current details, so check it rather than relying on a summary.
  • Elsewhere: many countries have their own late payment rules, which may apply automatically or only if your terms say so.

This guide is general information, not legal advice. If a large invoice is at stake, a short conversation with a local accountant or lawyer is cheap insurance.

Wording you can adapt

Payment is due within 14 days of the invoice date. Invoices unpaid after the due date may be charged a late fee of 1.5% of the outstanding balance per month (or a minimum of $25, whichever is greater), unless a different rate is required by local law.

Keep the language plain. If you need a place to put it, the notes or terms field on our free invoice generator is a good spot, and profession-specific pages such as the freelance writer invoice template work the same way.

Before you charge: a sensible escalation order

  1. Send a friendly reminder a few days before or right after the due date. Many late payments are simple oversights.
  2. Follow up politely with the invoice attached. Our late payment email templates cover each stage.
  3. Mention the fee if the invoice is still unpaid, pointing back to the agreed terms.
  4. Apply the fee and send an updated invoice or a separate fee invoice with a clear new total.
  5. Pause new work if your terms allow it, and consider formal collection options only as a last resort.

Should you actually charge it?

A late fee is a business decision as much as a financial one. Some freelancers keep the clause in every contract but waive it for good clients who pay late once. That keeps the leverage without damaging the relationship. Others use it strictly for clients with a history of delays. Either approach is fine as long as you are consistent and the terms were agreed up front.

Also think about prevention. Shorter payment terms, a deposit on larger projects, and invoicing promptly after delivery all reduce the need for fees in the first place. Our guide to your first freelance invoice covers the basics of getting that right.

A quick worked example

Suppose you invoice $2,000 due on the 1st, with a simple late fee of 1.5% per month. If the client pays one month late, the fee is $2,000 x 1.5% = $30, so they owe $2,030. If they pay three months late, the simple fee is $90. With monthly compounding it would be slightly higher, about $91.36. The difference is small over a few months, which is why many freelancers pick the simple version because it is easier to explain.

Key takeaways

  • Agree the fee in writing before the work begins and repeat it on every invoice.
  • Choose a structure you can explain in one sentence.
  • Check local limits on fees and interest, because they vary widely.
  • Remind politely first; apply the fee consistently afterwards.
  • Prevent late payment with clear due dates, deposits and prompt invoicing.
