How Many Billable Days Does a Freelancer Really Have? (Day Rate Guide)

Your day rate is only as good as the number of days you divide by. Most new freelancers divide their income goal by the 260 weekdays in a year and end up underpaid, because a large share of those days are not billable. This guide shows how to estimate your real billable days, with a worked example. Plug your own numbers into the freelance day rate calculator, then bill with the free invoice generator.

Why 260 days is the wrong denominator

A year has roughly 260 weekdays. You will not bill all of them. Holidays, public holidays and sick days remove some, and the rest of your working time goes to unpaid work: finding clients, sending quotes, bookkeeping, learning and chasing late payments. Sources that discuss this generally land well below 260; many quote something like 130 to 180 billable days, though the right figure depends on your field and how much you sell versus deliver. Treat any benchmark as a starting assumption, not a promise.

Step 1: Start from weekdays and subtract time off

Begin with 260 and remove the days you will not work. Use your own plan and your local calendar:

  • Holiday / vacation days: as many as you realistically intend to take.
  • Public holidays: check the count for your country or region.
  • Sick or unexpected days: budget some, because as a freelancer these are unpaid.

Example: 25 holiday days + 8 public holidays + 7 sick or buffer days = 40 days off. 260 − 40 = 220 available working days.

Step 2: Apply a utilization rate

Utilization is the share of available days you actually bill. The rest is admin, sales, learning and gaps between projects. Some calculators treat around 80% as healthy for an established freelancer; others suggest 50-70% is common once sales and admin are included, especially in the first year. If you are unsure, track your time for a month and use your real figure.

Example: 220 available days × 70% utilization = 154 billable days.

Step 3: Divide your revenue target by billable days

Your revenue target should cover your take-home goal, business expenses, and an allowance for taxes. Tax rules differ by country, so check local rules or ask an accountant before settling on a percentage. If you want a rough set-aside figure, try the tax set-aside calculator.

Example: You need $88,000 of revenue for the year.

  • Naive: 88,000 ÷ 260 = $338.46 per day.
  • Realistic: 88,000 ÷ 154 = $571.43 per day.

The realistic day rate is about 69% higher, and it is the one that actually reaches the target. Divide by 8 for a rough hourly equivalent: $571.43 ÷ 8 = $71.43 per hour. Cross-check against the hourly rate calculator.

Step 4: Handle days that are not full days

Real work rarely fits whole days. Common approaches:

  • Half-day minimum: bill a half day for anything under four hours, and say so in your terms.
  • Travel and prep: decide whether they are billable and write it down before the project starts.
  • Overruns: define what happens past the agreed days; see the retainer pricing guide for overage wording that works for ongoing work.

Step 5: Show days on the invoice

A clear invoice makes a day rate feel fair. Use one line per kind of work, with the quantity in days and the rate per day:

  • "Discovery workshop, 1 day @ $571.43"
  • "Design and build, 6 days @ $571.43"
  • "Half-day review, 0.5 day @ $571.43"

Pair this with sensible payment terms and, for longer jobs, a deposit or milestones. Your profession's invoice template can be a starting point.

How to measure your own utilization

Guesswork is fine for a first quote, but real data is better. For four weeks, log each working day as billable, partly billable or non-billable, and note what filled the non-billable time. Divide billable days by days worked to get your utilization. Many freelancers are surprised by how much time disappears into proposals, client emails and bookkeeping. Once you know the figure, you can either raise your rate to cover it or reduce the non-billable work, for example by using templates for quotes and a consistent invoice numbering system so admin takes minutes instead of hours.

New freelancers should expect lower utilization in the first year while the client base builds, so consider setting a higher day rate or a smaller income target until the pipeline fills. Revisit the calculation whenever your workload, expenses or time-off plans change.

Common mistakes

  • Assuming full utilization: pipeline gaps are normal; plan for them.
  • Forgetting unpaid time: quotes, emails and bookkeeping count as work you cannot bill.
  • Ignoring expenses: software, insurance, equipment and accounting should be inside the revenue target.
  • Never revisiting the number: review your real billable days every quarter and adjust.

Quick checklist

  1. Count weekdays and subtract holidays, public holidays and sick days.
  2. Pick a utilization rate you can defend, or measure it.
  3. Set a revenue target that includes expenses and a tax allowance (check local rules).
  4. Divide target by billable days.
  5. State minimums and overrun rules in your terms, then invoice in days.
