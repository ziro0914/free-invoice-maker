How to Price Jobs by the Square Foot (and Check You're Paid Enough)

Pricing by the square foot is the quickest way to quote painting, flooring, pressure washing, cleaning and similar area-based work. It is also an easy way to underpay yourself if you stop at the first number. This guide shows how to build a square-foot quote, how to sanity-check it against your hours, and how to show it clearly on an invoice. You can follow along with our price per square foot calculator.

Why price by the square foot

Clients like it because it is easy to compare, and you like it because you can quote from a measurement without writing a long proposal. It works best when the job divides into measurable units and the work is fairly uniform: a driveway, a deck, an open floor, a set of identical rooms. It works worst when prep, repairs or access change how long the job takes more than the area does.

The basic formula

  1. Measure the area. Length times width, in square feet. For several rooms or surfaces, add them up.
  2. Multiply by your rate per square foot. This gives you the labor price before extras.
  3. Add materials. If you supply paint, cleaner, underlayment or similar items, list them separately or build them into a stated rate, but be consistent.
  4. Apply a minimum charge. If the area-based labor is below your minimum, charge the minimum.

A worked example

A driveway is 30 ft by 20 ft, so the area is 600 sq ft. At a rate of 0.25 per sq ft, labor is 600 x 0.25 = 150. Your minimum charge is 100, so it does not apply here. Add 15 of supplies and the quote is 165. If you expect the job to take 3 hours, labor pay is 150 / 3 = 50 per hour. These numbers are illustrations, not market rates.

Where your rate comes from

Do not copy a number from a website. Trade-software blogs publish wide ranges for painting and flooring, and the ranges disagree with each other because region, surface, condition and job size all move the price. Treat them as rough context only. A more reliable way is to start from what you need to earn:

  • Decide your target hourly or day rate. If you are unsure, our hourly rate calculator and day rate calculator help.
  • Estimate how many hours a typical job takes for each 100 sq ft, including prep and cleanup, from your own past jobs.
  • Divide: rate per square foot = (hours per square foot) x (target hourly rate), then add margin for overhead such as insurance, fuel and equipment.

For example, if you usually clear 200 sq ft per hour and want 50 per hour, that is 50 / 200 = 0.25 per sq ft. Track your real times for a few jobs and adjust.

Check it against your hours

Always run the reverse check before you send a quote: quote divided by expected hours. If the answer is below your target, the square-foot rate is too low for that job, no matter how normal the rate sounds. Our effective hourly rate calculator shows what you really earn once unpaid time is counted, and the effective rate guide explains why this gap matters.

Use a minimum charge for small jobs

Small jobs still cost travel, setup and cleanup. A 40 sq ft job at 0.25 per sq ft is only 10, which would not cover the trip. A minimum charge fixes that. Many contractors bundle several small tasks into one visit so the minimum is paid once. State the minimum in your quote so it is never a surprise.

When square-foot pricing breaks down

  • Heavy prep or repair. Peeling paint, damaged subfloor or rot add hours that the area does not reflect. Price them as separate line items.
  • Removal and disposal. Taking out old flooring or hauling debris is separate work; list it on its own line.
  • Access and height. Stairs, ladders and multi-story work slow you down. Add a clearly labeled access line.
  • Very large jobs. Bigger areas can be done more efficiently, so some pros offer a lower rate above a size threshold. That is your call; just write it down.
  • Surprises mid-job. Put change requests in writing. See how to handle scope creep.

Put it on the invoice clearly

An area-based invoice is easiest to read when each line shows the measurement. For example: "Pressure washing, driveway, 600 sq ft at 0.25 per sq ft", then separate lines for supplies, removal or access, and any deposit already paid. If you took a deposit, see how to invoice a deposit. Sales tax or VAT treatment of labor and materials differs by place, so check your local rules or ask an accountant.

You can start from a ready layout in our pressure washing, painter or window cleaner invoice templates, then edit the quantities and rates. If you quote a fixed price rather than a per-unit one, compare it with our project price calculator.

A quick checklist before you send the quote

  • Measured area and how you measured it
  • Rate per square foot and what it includes
  • Materials: included or separate
  • Minimum charge, if any
  • Extra lines for prep, repairs, removal and access
  • Quote divided by expected hours, compared with your target
  • Payment terms and any deposit

Square-foot pricing is a starting point, not the whole answer. Use it to get to a number fast, then confirm the number pays you what you need. This is general information, not financial or tax advice.
