Effective Hourly Rate vs Billing Rate: What Freelancers Really Earn

Your billing rate is the number you put on the invoice. Your effective hourly rate is what you actually earn for every hour you work, including the hours nobody pays you for. The gap between the two is where many freelancers quietly lose money. This guide explains the difference, shows a worked example, and helps you set a billing rate that reaches the effective rate you want. Billing rate vs effective rate

  • Billing rate: the price per hour (or day) you quote and invoice.
  • Effective rate: your income for a period divided by all the hours you worked in it, billable and non-billable together.

Non-billable work is real work: finding clients, sending proposals, answering email, bookkeeping, chasing late payments, learning new tools and fixing your own website. Because those hours produce no invoice, your effective rate is almost always lower than your billing rate.

The formula

Effective hourly rate = (income - business costs) / (billable hours + non-billable hours).

A simpler version, ignoring costs, is: billing rate x (billable hours / total hours). Whether you subtract costs is up to you; just be consistent when you compare months.

A worked example

Say you bill $75 an hour and work 40 hours in a typical week. Of those, 24 hours are billable and 16 are admin, sales and other unpaid tasks.

  1. Revenue for the week: 24 x $75 = $1,800.
  2. Effective rate: $1,800 / 40 = $45 per hour.
  3. Billable share: 24 / 40 = 60%.

So a $75 rate behaves like a $45 wage for the time you give up. If you also pay for software, a co-working desk or insurance, the number drops further.

Working backwards: what should you bill?

Suppose you want to earn an effective $50 an hour and you expect 60% of your hours to be billable. Divide the target by the billable share:

  • $50 / 0.60 = $83.33 per hour.

If you can only bill 50% of your time, the same target needs $50 / 0.50 = $100 an hour. If you manage 70%, it needs about $71.43. The billable share has as much influence on your income as the rate itself.

What is a realistic billable share?

There is no universal figure. Guides you will find online suggest anything from roughly half to about two-thirds of working time being billable, and the answer depends on your field, how steady your clients are and how much you spend on sales. Treat any benchmark as a rough rule of thumb and measure your own for a few weeks instead. A simple time log with two categories, billable and everything else, is enough.

How to raise your effective rate

1. Raise the billing rate

The most direct lever. Even a modest increase on new clients lifts the effective rate on every billable hour. See our freelance hourly rate calculator for a target-income starting point.

2. Cut unpaid hours

Use templates for proposals and emails, batch admin into one block a week, and automate recurring invoices where you can. Reuse a saved invoice layout instead of rebuilding one each time.

3. Stop unpaid scope creep

Every extra revision you do not charge for adds hours without adding revenue. A written change process helps; see how to handle scope creep and the scope creep cost calculator.

4. Shorten the payment wait

Chasing invoices is unpaid time too. Clear payment terms, a deposit up front and polite reminders reduce it. Details in the deposit guide.

5. Move toward project, retainer or day pricing

If you are fast, hourly billing punishes you. A fixed fee or retainer lets efficiency raise your effective rate. Compare options with the project price calculator and the billable days guide.

Common mistakes when calculating it

  • Counting only invoiced hours. If you leave out the time spent on proposals, calls that never turned into work and admin, the number looks better than reality.
  • Ignoring unpaid revisions. Hours spent past the agreed scope belong in the total hours even though they earned nothing extra.
  • Mixing months. Income is lumpy. A single strong month can hide a weak quarter, so compare three months or more where you can.
  • Forgetting costs. Software, equipment, insurance and platform fees reduce what you keep. Decide whether your effective rate is before or after costs, and say so in your notes.
  • Comparing with an employee wage directly. Employees usually receive paid leave, sick pay and employer contributions, while freelancers fund those themselves. A fair comparison needs a higher freelance figure, though the exact gap depends on your country and situation.

None of these require special software. A spreadsheet or even a notebook with income, costs and hours per week is enough to start.

Do not forget tax

An effective rate before tax is not take-home pay. Self-employed people usually owe income tax and often additional social contributions, and the rules differ by country and change over time. Check your local rules or ask an accountant, and use the tax set-aside calculator for a rough planning figure only.

A quick monthly routine

  1. Total the month's income and business costs.
  2. Add up all hours worked, billable and not.
  3. Divide to get your effective rate and compare it with your target.
  4. If it falls short, pick one lever from the list above and change it next month.
