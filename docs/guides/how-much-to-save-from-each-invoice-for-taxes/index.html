How Much to Save From Each Invoice for Taxes (Simple Freelancer Routine)

When a client pays a freelance invoice, the full amount lands in your account, but not all of it is yours. Taxes, business costs and a buffer have to come out first. This guide gives a simple routine for deciding how much of each invoice to set aside, with a worked example. It is general information, not tax advice, and rules differ by country and state, so check local rules or ask a qualified accountant.

Why the full invoice total is not your income

Three things sit between an invoice total and money you can spend:

  • Business expenses such as software, equipment, subcontractors and (where allowed) a share of home-office costs.
  • Taxes, which are usually not withheld for you when you are self-employed.
  • A buffer for slow months, refunds and unexpected costs.

Most sources base the tax estimate on net profit (income minus expenses), not on the invoice total. That is why a flat percentage of every invoice is only a rough starting point.

What percentage do people commonly use?

There is no single correct number. Guides aimed at US freelancers commonly suggest somewhere around 25-35% of net profit, with the low end for lower earners in states without income tax and the high end for higher earners or high-tax states. The US self-employment tax rate is 15.3% (covering Social Security and Medicare, calculated on a reduced base of net earnings), and income tax comes on top of that. Other countries have very different systems, so treat any percentage as a placeholder until you check your own situation.

If you are unsure, many freelancers round the percentage up. Having a little extra left over is easier to live with than an unexpected bill.

A simple routine: three buckets per payment

  1. Tax bucket: move your chosen percentage into a separate savings account the day the payment arrives.
  2. Expense bucket: if you pay business costs from a different account, move your typical expense share too.
  3. Owner pay: the rest is what you can pay yourself.

Doing it per payment, not at year end, is the habit that matters most. Money you never see in your spending account is money you do not spend by accident.

Worked example

Say you invoice a client $2,000 and expect about 20% of your income to go to business costs over the year, and you decide to set aside 30% of net profit for taxes.

StepCalculationAmount
Invoice paid$2,000.00
Expected expenses (20%)2,000 x 0.20$400.00
Net profit2,000 - 400$1,600.00
Tax set-aside (30%)1,600 x 0.30$480.00
Left for you1,600 - 480$1,120.00

So roughly 24% of the invoice total (480 / 2,000) goes to tax in this example. Your numbers will differ, which is why the next step is worth doing.

Quarterly payments and deadlines

In the US, people who expect to owe a certain amount of tax (the IRS commonly cites $1,000) generally make estimated payments during the year rather than one lump sum. Other countries have their own schedules, such as payments on account in the UK. Check your tax authority's website for dates and thresholds, and put them in your calendar. Your tax bucket is what pays these.

What if income is irregular?

Freelance income rarely arrives evenly. A practical approach is to keep the same percentage on every payment, so big months build a cushion that covers quiet ones. At the end of each quarter, compare what is in your tax account with what you expect to owe, and adjust the percentage up or down for the next quarter. If you have a large one-off payment, run the calculator again with the higher expected profit, because higher income can push part of it into a higher bracket. Keep notes on what you assumed so you can explain your numbers to an accountant later.

Common mistakes

  • Treating the deposit as income to spend. Set aside tax from deposits too.
  • Forgetting expenses are only deductible if they qualify. Keep receipts and check local rules.
  • Using last year's percentage forever. Revisit it when your income or location changes.
  • Mixing business and personal money. A separate account makes everything easier at tax time.

Quick checklist

  1. Pick a starting percentage and round up if unsure.
  2. Open a separate savings account for tax.
  3. Move the percentage the day each payment arrives.
  4. Check the real number with the calculator or an accountant.
  5. Review the percentage at least once a year.
