2/10 Net 30 Explained: Should Freelancers Offer Early Payment Discounts?

Terms like "2/10 net 30" show up on invoices all the time, but many freelancers are not sure what they mean or whether offering one is a good idea. This guide explains how an early payment discount works, how to work out what it really costs you, and how to word it on an invoice. For the basics of due dates, see our guide to invoice payment terms.

What "2/10 net 30" means

The shorthand has three numbers. The first is the discount percentage, the second is the number of days the discount is available, and "net" plus a number is the final due date. So 2/10 net 30 means the client may take 2% off if they pay within 10 days; otherwise the full amount is due within 30 days. If you are still deciding between standard terms, compare Net 30 vs Net 15 first.

A worked example

Say you send a 1,000 invoice with 2/10 net 30 terms.

Client paysAmount you receive
Within 10 days980 (1,000 minus 2%, which is 20)
Day 11 to day 301,000

You give up 20 to get paid up to 20 days sooner (the gap between day 10 and day 30). Whether that is a good trade depends on how much those 20 days are worth to you.

The hidden cost: an annualized rate

A 2% discount sounds small, but it is paid for speeding things up by only 20 days. A common way to compare it with interest is: discount divided by what you still receive, multiplied by 365 divided by the days you speed up payment.

  • 2 / 98 = 0.0204
  • 365 / 20 = 18.25
  • 0.0204 x 18.25 is roughly 37% a year (simple, 365-day basis)

Some textbooks use a 360-day year, which gives a slightly lower number, and compounding makes it higher. Either way, it is a lot more than most credit costs, which is why the discount is attractive to clients with spare cash. A smaller discount such as 1/10 net 30 works out to roughly half of that. You can run your own numbers with the early payment discount calculator.

Is it worth it for you?

Compare the annualized cost with what your own money costs. If you would otherwise borrow on a credit card or overdraft at a rate well below 37%, the discount costs you more than waiting does. In the 1,000 example above, waiting 20 days at a 12% yearly cost of money costs about 6.44 in interest, while the discount costs 20.

The discount can still make sense in some situations:

  • You have a real cash-flow gap and the alternative is expensive financing.
  • Your client is a business with an accounts-payable team that processes invoices on a schedule. Sources differ, but many say discounts suit these clients best.
  • You have a client who habitually pays late and you want a positive nudge instead of a penalty.

It is usually a poor fit when your clients already pay on time, when your margins are thin, or when the invoice is small enough that the discount barely changes anything. Treat this as a rule of thumb, not financial advice; your situation may differ.

Discount or late fee?

A discount rewards early payers; a late fee penalizes late ones. Some freelancers use both. Be careful with fees: rules about late interest differ by country and sometimes by contract type, so check local rules. Our guide on how to charge late fees and the invoice late fee calculator cover that side.

How to word it on your invoice

Put the terms near the total so they are hard to miss. Plain sentences are clearer than shorthand for clients who have never seen "2/10 net 30":

Payment terms: Net 30, due 15 December. A 2% discount applies if paid in full by 25 November.

A few tips:

  1. Write actual dates, not just day counts, so nobody argues about when the clock started.
  2. State what the discount applies to. Is it the whole invoice, or only the services before tax and shipping? Be explicit, and check how tax works in your location.
  3. Say what happens after the window. The full amount is due by the final date.
  4. Decide how you treat short payments. If a client deducts the discount after the window has passed, a polite reminder with the original terms usually settles it.
  5. Put it in the contract or proposal as well, so the invoice is not the first time the client sees it.

Alternatives that cost less

  • Deposits upfront. Taking part of the fee before work starts improves cash flow without a discount. See our guide to deposit invoices.
  • Milestone billing. Smaller invoices on a schedule mean less money outstanding at any time. See milestone billing.
  • Shorter terms. Net 15 instead of Net 30 gets you paid sooner without giving anything away.
  • Faster invoicing. Sending the invoice the day work is done is free and shortens the wait.

Checklist before you offer a discount

  1. Work out the annualized cost with the calculator.
  2. Compare it with your real cost of money.
  3. Start small (for example 1%) and see whether clients respond.
  4. Write the exact dates and scope on the invoice.
  5. Track how many clients take it, so you can stop if it just reduces your income.
